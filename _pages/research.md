I work at the Satellite Geodesy Laboratory of the Interdisciplinary Centre for Water Research, Indian Institute of Science, Bengaluru. This page describes what I work on and where I want to take it.

## Closing the sea level budget

Sea level is easy to measure and hard to explain. My central work is closing the sea level budget: accounting for every contribution to the observed change, from melting ice and the warming of the ocean to the water moving through rivers, soils and aquifers, until the parts add up to the whole. For the modern period I do this with state-of-the-art geodetic observations, combining GRACE and GRACE-FO gravimetry, satellite altimetry and Argo float data. I am equally interested in the years before these tools existed. Satellite altimetry began only in 1993, so for earlier decades I approach the budget from the hydrological side, asking how land water storage has changed. I am also revisiting how tide-gauge based global sea level and paleo sea level records can be re-examined, to uncover signals that earlier analyses may have left hidden.

## Ice sheets and the sea level system

Sea level does not rise evenly when ice melts. Water that leaves glaciated land, such as ice sheets and glaciers, and non-glaciated land, such as rivers, soils and aquifers, enters the ocean, and the ocean then redistributes it. Gravity, the rotation of the Earth and the deformation of the solid Earth all shape where that water ends up, which produces patterns known as sea level fingerprints. I work on the ice sheet and sea level system as a whole: how mass leaves the land, how the ocean spreads it, and how we can read the fingerprints in observations to trace where and how fast ice is changing.

## Coupled forcings and sea level variability

Ice is only one part of the story. The atmosphere, land and ocean are coupled, and each one forces the others. Winds and pressure push water around the ocean, rainfall and evaporation change what the land stores, and ocean circulation and heat uptake change sea level from place to place. I study how these interactions together drive sea level variability at global and regional scales, so that a regional change can be traced back to its causes instead of being treated as a single number.

## Past ice sheets and future projections

I am deeply interested in reconstructing the climate of the past, in particular paleo sea level rise and paleo ice sheet mass change. Past episodes are among the few long records we have of how ice sheets and the ocean respond to a changing climate. I want to use them to improve projections of ice sheet mass loss and of sea level for the year 2100.

## A side project: a virtual rover for the deep ocean

Alongside my PhD, I am working on an ambitious project as a hobby: a virtual robotic rover imagined as an advanced successor to the Argo float. Argo floats have transformed how we observe temperature and salinity, but most of their profiles stop at about 2,000 m. In my simulation I recreate the dynamics of the ocean, including waves, over the whole water column, and I test how such a rover could move through it. The questions I am exploring are how to sample the ocean better than Argo does, in the vertical and across space, how to plan the rover's paths, and how to measure temperature, salinity and other variables where we know least. My focus is the deep ocean below 2,000 m, where observations are sparse and where climate change has started to leave its mark. The project is still at the simulation stage.
